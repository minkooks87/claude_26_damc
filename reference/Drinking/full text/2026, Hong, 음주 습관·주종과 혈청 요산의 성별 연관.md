# Sex-Specific Association of Drinking Habit and Alcohol Beverage Type With Serum Uric Acid in a Healthy Population

## Abstract


### Background

This study aimed to assess the sex-specific effects of alcohol consumption on serum uric acid (SUA) levels regarding alcohol amount, beverage types and drinking habits in Koreans.


### Methods

We evaluated 17,011 adults who underwent health examinations at Samsung Medical Center between January 2011 and June 2016. Alcohol intake was measured in a standard drink unit (SDU), contained 8 g of ethanol. Individuals were categorized into six groups according to their alcohol intake from none to heavy drinkers. A dominant beverage was defined if it accounted for more than 75% of total alcohol intake. Multivariable linear regression models were used to investigate alcohol effect on SUA levels.


### Results

The mean age was 51.67 ± 7.10 years, with 53.4% of men. Higher total alcohol intake was associated with higher SUA levels (P for trend < 0.001). This dose-dependent association was observed for all beverage types. Among men, heavy drinking showed a larger effect on SUA levels in the beer-dominant group (0.88 [95% confidence interval {CI}, 0.36–1.41] mg/dL per SDU) than in other dominant groups. Little soju intake in men and light beer intake in women were associated with elevated SUA levels (0.12 [95% CI, 0.03–0.20] and 0.34 [95% CI, 0.18–0.50] mg/dL per SDU, respectively). The increment in SUA levels was higher in subjects with a body mass index (BMI) < 25 kg/m2 than in those with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 across most beverage types. The effect of soju intake on SUA levels was more than twice as high in women compared to men (0.07 [95% CI, 0.02–0.11] versus 0.03 [95% CI, 0.02–0.04] mg/dL per SDU) in the BMI < 25 kg/m2 group.


### Conclusion

Dose-dependent associations between SUA levels and alcohol consumption were consistently observed across different beverage types, drinking habits, sex, and BMI, even at low dose alcohol intake. These findings may aid healthcare providers to offer personalized guidance on alcohol consumption for individuals with hyperuricemia.


## Graphical Abstract




## INTRODUCTION

Alcohol consumption is a major dietary cause of hyperuricemia and gout with increased uric acid production and decreased excretion implicated in the pathogenesis.1234

Various guidelines for gout management have recommended modifications in diet and lifestyle, including alcohol restriction.567 While many studies advise alcohol abstinence or limitation for hyperuricemia or gout, there exists conflicting evidence among studies.38910 Some studies suggest that wine protects against serum uric acid (SUA) elevation,311 whereas others indicate its minimal impact or potential risk.121314 Although a recent study suggested that the relationship between SUA levels and alcohol intake varied according to the type of alcohol consumed,15 comprehensive research on drinking behaviors and dietary habits is needed for precise guidance on alcohol moderation.

Due to inherited genetic variants in alcohol metabolism, ethnicity can play a significant role in the development of hyperuricemia.16171819 In addition, ethnic groups have different drinking styles: amount, alcoholic beverage types, mixing-alcohol consumption habits, and co-served foods. Most studies were conducted in limited countries including the US, Japan, and Singapore.111520 Thus, studies for various ethnic populations are needed. Additionally, the cause of ethanol-induced hyperuricemia is multifactorial. The alcoholic content and high purine levels in certain alcoholic beverages seem to influence the rise in SUA levels. Beer is known to have a particularly potent effect on SUA concentrations. Soju is one of the most popular alcoholic beverages in Korea and has gained popularity worldwide due to Korean Wave.21 The alcohol content of soju varies, but recently, soju with a low alcohol content of less than 20% has become more popular. Also, the amount of purine was high in beer, but not detected in distilled liquors such as soju.22

There was no research investigating the sex-specific association between SUA levels and alcoholic beverage consumption considering the dose-response. We aimed to investigate the sex-specific safe margin of individual alcoholic beverage consumption on SUA levels in Koreans while taking into account drinking habits as well as alcohol amount and beverage types.


## METHODS


### Study design and population

This retrospective study was conducted on 61,487 adults over the age of 18 who underwent health examinations at Samsung Medical Center (SMC) between January 2011 and June 2016 (Fig. 1). The routine health examinations consisted of self-reported questionnaires regarding medical history and lifestyle factors, physical examinations including anthropometric measurements and laboratory tests. A variety of investigations have linked hypertension, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease, including heart failure to increase SUA levels.23 Furthermore, the consumption of certain drugs increases SUA levels (low dose aspirin, ethambutol, and pyrazinamide).24 Based on these findings, exclusions were made for individuals with the following baseline characteristics: estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) > 9%; history of heart disease, cancer, and stroke; use of aspirin medication; total energy intake (kcal) < 800 or > 4,000. We also excluded all subjects with missing values for any variable, assuming the data were missing completely at random, as no unusual missing patterns were observed. Finally, a total of 17,011 subjects were included in this study.

The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.


### Data collection

From DARWIN-C, the SMC clinical data warehouse, and the DR.H (Data Registry of Health), we retrieved participants’ data including age, sex, anthropometric measurements, laboratory results, and medical history. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). Blood samples for SUA, fasting glucose, HbA1c, eGFR, creatinine and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were collected after 12 hours of overnight fasting. Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined as fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or as treated with diabetes medication. Hypertension was defined as treated with hypertension medication, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg.

During the routine examinations, participants were requested to complete self-reported food frequency questionnaires (FFQs) regarding their dietary habits over the previous 3 months, which was a semiquantitative form designed and validated in Korea.25 Registered dietitians with a minimum of two years of experience verified the answers to the FFQ. Total energy intake, alcohol-free energy intake and nutrient calculations were determined using the Can-pro 3.0 database (Nutritional Assessment Program 2008; Korean Nutrition Society, Seoul, Korea).


### Assessment of alcohol consumption and definition of dominant alcoholic beverage types

From the FFQ, the daily alcohol consumption was assessed for each of five alcoholic beverage types that were predominantly consumed in South Korea: beer, soju (Korean liquor), wine, whiskey, and cheongju (rice wine of Korean origin). A standard drink unit (SDU) was defined as an intake of 8 g of pure ethanol for Koreans.26 One SDU corresponds to 220 mL of 4.5% beer, 50 mL of 20% soju, 85 mL of 12% wine, 25 mL of 40% whiskey, and 70 mL of 15% cheongju.2728 Alcohol consumption for each beverage was assessed using SDU per day. Intakes for cheongju and whiskey were combined and labeled as “others” due to relatively small amounts. Total alcohol intake was calculated as sum of the daily intakes of all alcoholic beverages. Participants were categorized into six intake groups according to their alcohol intake (in SDU per day): non- (0), almost none (0.01–0.49), little (0.50–0.99), light (1.00–1.99), moderate (2.00–3.99) and heavy (≥ 4.00) drinkers.

It is difficult to evaluate the isolated effects of each beverage type on SUA levels since Korean culture tends to drink a mixture of various alcoholic beverages. We defined a dominant alcoholic beverage for each participant when it constituted more than 75% of total alcohol intake.15 Participants were grouped according to their dominant beverage type whereas those with no dominant type were classified into a mixed-alcohol group. Cheongju- and whiskey-dominant groups were excluded from the analysis due to small sample size and data sparsity.

To take drinking habits into account, we defined another unit for alcohol intake called one serving per occasion (SPO) (Supplementary Table 1).


### Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics by sex were described as means with standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables, and as counts with percentages for categorical variables. Differences between drinkers and non-drinkers were assessed using the absolute value of the standardized mean difference with values greater than 0.2 considered to indicate considerable differences.

Sex-specific associations between alcohol intake and SUA levels were examined, and interactions between alcohol intake and sex were tested using a linear regression model. Multivariable linear regression models were used to investigate the association between alcohol intake (in SDU per day) and SUA levels in the whole population. All results were presented as regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Three nested models were considered: 1) crude coefficients with no adjustment; 2) age-adjusted coefficients; and 3) fully-adjusted coefficients with additional adjustments for well-known confounders, including BMI, creatinine, LDL-C, hypertension, DM, alcohol-free energy intake and total protein intake.112930 When alcohol intake of each beverage was analyzed, those of other beverages were adjusted for in the fully-adjusted models. To capture nonlinear associations, alcohol intake was treated as a categorical variable with six intake groups for the intake of each alcoholic beverage as well as the total intake. Additionally, to assess linear associations, we used alcohol intake as ethanol content and performed fully-adjusted multivariable linear regression models. Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether the association of alcohol intake on SUA levels could be modified by BMI (< 25 vs ≥ 25 kg/m2) in each sex-specific analysis. Interactions between alcohol intake and BMI groups were tested using the linear regression model.

We also investigated the association between alcohol intake and SUA levels in each dominant beverage group in the same manner. In addition, the interaction between alcohol intake with dominant alcoholic beverage was examined. To explore dietary patterns within each dominant group, we compared average intakes of dietary nutrients including calorie, protein, fat, calcium, vitamin C, and fiber.

All tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was declared with a P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (version 4.1.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).


### Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Samsung Medical Center approved this study (IRB No. 2021-02-077) and waived the requirement for informed consent given the retrospective nature of the study.


## RESULTS


### Baseline characteristics

The analysis included 17,011 participants, with 30.4% non-drinkers and 69.6% drinkers. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Table 1 outlines the demographics and clinical characteristics of male and female drinkers based on total alcohol intake. Almost-none and little drinkers accounted for 23.5% of men and 80.5% of women, while the proportions of moderate to heavy drinkers were significantly different between men and women (58.3% vs 7.6%). Total alcohol intake was higher in men than in women (mean ± SD: 3.63 ± 3.53 vs 0.73 ± 1.09 SDU per day, respectively). On average, more than half of total alcohol intake in men came from soju (mean ± SD: 2.20 ±2.55). Soju intake increased rapidly compared to other alcoholic beverages as total alcohol intake increased, and the mean ± SD soju intake was the highest at 4.76 ± 2.95 SDU per day among heavy drinkers. We observed increasing trends in the means of age, BMI, SUA, energy intake, and total protein intake in men as alcohol consumption increased from almost none to heavy drinking (P values for trend < 0.001). In women, significant differences were observed only in age, SUA, LDL-C and total protein intake.

### Association between SUA levels and alcohol intakes in men

SUA levels increased as alcohol intake increased (P for trend < 0.001). Even small alcohol drinking, specifically above 0.5 SDU of soju per day, was significantly associated with increased SUA levels (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 3). SUA levels were positively associated with alcohol intake in a different degree according to dominant alcoholic beverage groups (Table 2). Compared to the soju-dominant and mixed-alcohol groups (0.29 [95% CI, 0.14 to 0.43] mg/dL and 0.17 [95% CI, 0.08 to 0.26] mg/dL, respectively), heavy drinking showed a significantly larger effect on SUA levels in the beer-dominant group (0.88 [95% CI, 0.36 to 1.41] mg/dL).

The effects of alcohol intake as the ethanol content on SUA levels were compared (Fig. 3). The positive association was observed for the total intake and across all types of alcoholic beverages. The wine effect showed the largest association (0.06 [95% CI, 0.01 to 0.11] mg/dL) compared to other beverage intakes. In addition, SUA levels were positively associated with soju intake in both BMI subgroups (0.03 [95% CI, 0.02 to 0.04] mg/dL and 0.02 [95% CI, 0.01 to 0.04] mg/dL, respectively). Regardless of BMI subgroup, the magnitudes of association for each alcoholic beverage type were similar. To consider drinking habits, we reinterpreted the results using one SPO (the sex-stratified average alcohol intake in drinkers for each alcoholic beverage, see Supplementary Table 1 for detail). The wine effect was the largest in the one SDU (Fig. 3), while it was the smallest in the one SPO (Supplementary Fig. 3). In contrast, the soju effect was the largest in the one SPO.

### Association between SUA levels and alcohol intakes in women

SUA levels increased as alcohol intake increased (P for trend = 0.001). The positive association between SUA levels and alcohol intake was significant when consuming above 1 SDU of beer and total alcohol per day (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 4). In the dominant alcoholic beverage groups, the mixed-alcohol group showed a significant effect of moderate (0.29 [95% CI, 0.14 to 0.44] mg/dL) and heavy drinking (0.68 [95% CI, 0.42 to 0.95] mg/dL) whereas other dominant groups did not show significance (Table 2).

Considering the ethanol content, beer intake showed the largest association (0.11 [95% CI, 0.06 to 0.16] mg/dL), and a similar result was observed for beer intake in BMI < 25 kg/m2 group (0.11 [95% CI, 0.07 to 0.17] mg/dL). However, all types of alcoholic beverages did not show significance in BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 group (Fig. 3). Contrary to SDU, wine effect was relatively smaller compared to other alcoholic beverages (Supplementary Fig. 3).


## DISCUSSION

There is no consensus on the exact relationship between alcohol consumption and hyperuricemia/gout,3313233 because very few comprehensive assessments were conducted on the effects of different types of alcoholic beverages and drinking behaviors on SUA levels.1115 This study identified a nonlinear dose-dependent positive association between SUA levels and alcohol consumption. Even a small amount of soju, equivalent to 0.5 SDU, was significantly associated with increased SUA levels in men. Similarly, light consumption of beer, equivalent to 1 SDU, showed a significant association in women. Wine intake was associated with increased SUA levels based on ethanol content, similar to findings in a recent study,15 and dose-response analyses showed a significant impact among heavy drinkers in the overall population (Figs. 2 and 3, Supplementary Table 5).

There are few studies that have defined the SDU based on the same ethanol content.1526 When comparing the association between SUA levels and alcohol intake from different alcoholic beverages, it is important to explain other variations among beverages other than ethanol content by using the SDU. We used the SDU defined for Koreans, which contains a smaller amount of ethanol (8 g) than the units used in previous studies. Considering the reported non-linear dose-response on health conditions,343536 we categorized participants according to alcohol intake into six groups from ‘almost-none (< 4 g)’ to ‘heavy (≥ 32 g)’ to obtain practical implication on low- as well as high-dose drinkers. There have been many studies indicating beneficial effects of light alcohol consumption on cardiovascular risk with a J-shaped relationship, but there is no evidence regarding whether light alcohol consumption is beneficial for SUA levels.373839 Our findings show for the first time that even very small amounts of alcohol intake (less than 1 SDU) are associated with increased SUA levels. There is no safe level of alcohol consumption. In general, diet and lifestyle modifications have a limited impact on lowering serum urate levels and cannot replace pharmacologic treatments. Adhering to stringent dietary modifications, such as low-purine diets and reduced alcohol intake, can result in a reduction of serum urate levels by approximately 10–15% (equivalent to about 1 mg/dL).40 In this study, heavy alcohol consumption among women in the mixed-alcohol group resulted in an increase in SUA levels by 0.68 mg/dL (Table 2). This finding suggests a significant effect, considering the modest dietary influence on serum urate levels. Previous studies have rarely detailed exactly how much serum urate levels change according to the type or amount of alcohol consumed. Consequently, our findings may assist clinicians in offering more precise and practical dietary recommendations for patients with hyperuricemia.

Each alcoholic beverage had a different effect on SUA levels for several reasons. First, the ingredients of alcoholic beverage may differ by beverage types.15 Beer is known to have a special capacity to elevate SUA levels due to its higher purine content, in contrast to other beverages like soju and wine.4142 Our study reconfirmed that the effect of beer intake on SUA levels was higher in women as shown in previous studies.1115 Second, individuals have different food preference that may be associated with alcoholic beverage preferences. Protein intake was higher in the soju-dominant and mixed-alcohol groups in men and the beer-dominant group in women (Supplementary Fig. 4).

There are sex differences in the alcohol intake effect on SUA levels as well as in drinking amounts and habits. More than half of the women do not consume alcohol while almost 90% of men do. Men preferred soju-dominant and mixed-alcohol drinking, whereas women had no specific preference. The alcohol intake effect on SUA levels was larger in women than in men as seen in the previous studies.1115 Our findings revealed the same result in the total alcohol intake group, indicating that men exhibited increased SUA levels starting from moderate intake (0.10 mg/dL; Supplementary Table 3), whereas women showed increases even with light intake (0.12 mg/dL; Supplementary Table 4). This could be attributed to differences in hormones or body composition affecting urate metabolism.434445 Additionally, previous studies have reported that women are generally more vulnerable to the effects of alcohol in various diseases than men,46474849 and similar to our findings, this may contribute to increases in SUA levels. Thus, sex difference should be considered when exploring the association between alcohol and SUA levels.

Alcohol intake and excess adiposity are known to be associated with SUA levels. Obesity increases urate production and decreases renal urate excretion. It has been postulated that the effect of alcohol consumption on SUA levels may vary by BMI.505152 Our study suggests lower alcohol effects in the obese group (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) than in the non-obese group across beer, soju, and total alcohol intakes. A stronger alcohol-SUA association was seen in non-obese women, highlighting BMI’s varying impact (Supplementary Table 6). Higher BMI may obscure the potential deleterious effect of alcohol on SUA levels, since the higher BMI group showed increased SUA levels. This suggests that obese patients with hyperuricemia should be advised to modify both BMI and alcohol consumption.

One serving unit was introduced to indicate the total amount of alcohol intake consumed at a single instance. Beer and soju have higher effects on SUA levels than wine in one SPO whereas wine has the highest effect in one SDU. This implicates that beer and soju can show large effect on SUA level due to large amount of consumption per occasion although they seem to have a lower effect per ethanol content than wine. Therefore, reducing the size of one serving should be promoted in patient care.

This study has several strengths. First, this is the first study to analyze the association of SUA levels depending on alcoholic beverage types considering dose-response effects, dominant alcoholic beverage effect, sex, and BMI in a large Korean population. To consider drinking habits, we conducted analyses based on one SPO and compared dietary nutrients. Second, we included soju, the most popular alcoholic beverage among Koreans. Recently, soju and Korean food have gained popularity worldwide due to the Korean Wave,215354555657 so it is meaningful to study soju effects on SUA levels. Finally, Korean culture tends to drink a mixture of various alcoholic beverages. We analyzed the isolated effect of individual alcoholic beverages by careful adjustment with alcohol amounts of other beverages.

However, there are also several limitations. First, the inherent biases of the retrospective observational study design may exist and have some influence on our findings. Second, the sample size was insufficient for the soju- and wine-dominant groups in women, as well as the wine-dominant group in men. Therefore, findings from the analyses of certain dominant alcoholic beverage groups and other smaller subgroups may lack sufficient reliability. To address this limitation, future research should focus on increasing sample sizes to ensure more robust and generalizable results. Third, detailed information on the food intakes co-served with alcoholic drinks was not available in this study.

Finally, the participants were Korean adults who underwent comprehensive health examinations, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to individuals with gout or other disease populations with different demographic or clinical characteristics. Specifically, alcohol metabolism and its effects on serum urate levels may differ in patients with gout. Furthermore, patterns and behaviors related to alcohol consumption may vary across different populations or ethnic groups, underscoring the necessity for further research in more diverse cohorts.

This study found consistent dose-dependent associations between SUA levels and alcohol consumption, across all types of alcoholic beverages in Koreans. Emphasizing the importance of considering sex, beverage types, drinking habits, and BMI, these findings can help healthcare providers to offer personalized guidance on alcohol consumption for individuals with hyperuricemia.
